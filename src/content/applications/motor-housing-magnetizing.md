## Where housing magnetizing is used

Housing (or stator) magnetizing is used wherever a PMDC motor has its magnets fixed inside a steel shell. Typical examples are automotive wiper, blower, window-lift and starter motors, small DC motors in appliances and pumps, and many industrial DC motors. The magnets are usually ferrite arcs held in place with adhesive or spring clips.

## How it works

A [motor housing magnetizing fixture](/products/magnetizing-fixtures-coils/motor-housing-magnetizing-fixture/) is shaped to fit inside the housing, with conductors arranged to create the required poles. A [capacitor discharge magnetizer](/products/magnetizing-machines/magnet-charging-machine/) fires a pulse of a few milliseconds through it, magnetizing the arcs radially from the inside, while the steel housing completes the magnetic path.

### Two-pole and four-pole housings

Most small PMDC motors use two poles, and larger designs often use four. The pole count and arc positions are built into the fixture, and the housing is located against a stop or key so that every part sits the same way.

### Why magnetize after assembly

Magnetized arcs are hard to fit: they snap to the housing wall, chip on contact and attract steel particles that later cause noise and wear. Fitting unmagnetized arcs and magnetizing the complete housing avoids this and makes automatic assembly practical.

## Getting a consistent result

- **Alignment:** the fixture poles must line up with the magnet arcs. An offset pole shifts the motor's magnetic axis, which can affect commutation and noise.
- **Field strength:** ferrite typically needs roughly 1–1.5 T to saturate, and the fixture must deliver this through any clearance between fixture and magnet.
- **Cycle rate:** fixture cooling and capacitor recharge time decide how many housings can be magnetized per hour.
- **Checks:** peak-current monitoring on every pulse and a gauss reading at each pole help catch missed or weak parts.
