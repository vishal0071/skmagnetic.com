## Charging magnets in-house

Many ferrite and NdFeB magnets leave the sintering and grinding stages unmagnetized. Whether you make magnets or trade them, charging is often the last step before dispatch, and doing it in-house means you control the timing, the polarity and the result.

The standard setup for loose magnets is a [magnet charging machine](/products/magnetizing-machines/magnet-charging-machine/) paired with a [magnetizing coil](/products/magnetizing-fixtures-coils/magnetizing-coil/). The magnet sits in the coil bore and is charged through its thickness by a pulse lasting a few milliseconds. Our explainer on [how a capacitor discharge magnetizer works](/blog/how-capacitor-discharge-magnetizer-works/) covers the principle.

## Common jobs for a charging setup

### Charging before dispatch

Stock is held unmagnetized for easy storage and counting, then charged to order. Shipping unmagnetized stock also avoids the special packing that magnetized material can need for air freight.

### Testing and sampling

Charging a few pieces from a batch and measuring them with a gauss meter, or a fluxmeter and Helmholtz coil, is a quick way to compare a new lot against a known good reference before a large order is magnetized.

### Re-charging stock

Magnets that have been partly demagnetized by heat, by strong opposing fields or by careless storage can usually be brought back to full strength. Our page on [magnet recharging](/applications/magnet-recharging/) explains what can and cannot be recovered.

## Getting the direction right

Most sintered magnets are anisotropic: they reach full strength only when magnetized along the direction they were oriented in during pressing. Charging them in any other direction gives a weak result. Isotropic magnets, such as many bonded magnets, can be magnetized in any direction, including multi-pole patterns made with a shaped fixture rather than a coil.
