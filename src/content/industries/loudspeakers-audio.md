## How speaker magnetizing works

A moving-coil loudspeaker has a magnet held between steel plates and a pole piece, forming a magnetic circuit with a narrow gap for the voice coil. Many manufacturers glue this circuit together with the magnet unmagnetized. The complete assembly, or the finished speaker, is then placed in a coil or fixture and charged by a [speaker magnetizer](/products/magnetizing-machines/speaker-magnetizer/). The pulse lasts only a few milliseconds.

## Ferrite and neodymium speakers

### Ferrite ring magnets

Most conventional woofers and full-range drivers use a ferrite ring magnet outside the voice coil. Ferrite saturates at a comparatively low field, roughly 1–1.5 T, but large rings need a coil with a big enough bore and a machine with enough stored energy.

### Neodymium magnets

Compact and lightweight drivers often use neodymium (NdFeB) magnets, usually inside the voice coil. NdFeB needs a much stronger field, typically around 2.5–3.5 T, so the magnetizer and coil must be specified for it. Our guide to [magnetizing field requirements](/blog/magnetizing-field-requirements/) explains why.

## Practical points for a speaker line

- **Assembly stage:** some lines magnetize the bare magnet assembly before the frame and cone are fitted, while others magnetize the finished speaker. The coil or fixture is designed around whichever stage you choose.
- **Polarity:** every magnet must be magnetized in the same direction so the speaker's acoustic polarity matches its terminal markings.
- **Location:** a simple nest that holds the assembly centred in the coil gives repeatable results.
- **Cycle time:** fixture cooling and capacitor recharge time set how many speakers can be magnetized per hour.
- **Checks:** a gauss reading in the gap, or a sensitivity test on the finished speaker, confirms the result.

The same machine is useful for [re-magnetizing speakers](/applications/magnet-recharging/) that come back for rework or repair.
