## What is a capacitor discharge magnetizer?

A capacitor discharge (CD) magnetizer is the most widely used type of industrial magnetizer. It charges a **bank of capacitors** to a set voltage and then discharges it through a **magnetizing coil or fixture** using a high-current switch. The current rises to thousands of amperes for a few milliseconds, and the coil turns that current into an intense magnetic field. Because the energy arrives as one short impulse, the same machine is also called an impulse magnetizer or a pulse magnetizer.

The pulse is so short that the coil carries enormous current without overheating — which is how a machine on a normal power supply can create fields strong enough to saturate even [NdFeB magnets](/blog/magnetizing-field-requirements/).

## Why manufacturers magnetize in-house

Many manufacturers buy magnets **unmagnetized** and charge them after assembly. This avoids the problems of handling strong magnets on the line — parts snapping together, chipped magnets and steel swarf sticking to every surface — and makes sure each finished product is fully magnetized. A magnet charger machine at the end of the line becomes the single point where every part receives the same, controlled pulse.

Typical examples include [BLDC rotors](/products/magnetizing-machines/rotor-magnetizer/), DC motor housings, loudspeaker magnet assemblies and magnets for sensors.

## Choosing the right magnetizer

### Energy and voltage

The energy rating (in kilojoules) and maximum voltage must be enough to drive the required field into your part through the chosen fixture. Larger parts, higher-coercivity magnets and multi-pole patterns all need more energy.

### Fixture or coil

A [magnetizing coil](/products/magnetizing-fixtures-coils/magnetizing-coil/) suits loose magnets magnetized through their thickness. Rotors, housings and multi-pole parts need a part-specific [magnetizing fixture](/products/magnetizing-fixtures-coils/multipole-magnetizing-fixture/).

### Production rate and monitoring

For production use, consider cycle time, fixture cooling and whether you need peak-current monitoring to confirm that every part received a full pulse. Our guide on [how to choose a magnetizer](/blog/how-to-choose-magnetizer/) covers these points in detail.

## What decides the price of a magnet charging machine?

There is no single list price, because every machine is configured around the parts it will magnetize. Two machines that look alike from the outside can differ widely in what they contain. These are the main cost drivers:

| Cost driver | How it affects the price |
| --- | --- |
| **Energy rating** | Larger capacitor banks and chargers — for big parts, NdFeB grades or batch charging — cost more than compact machines for small ferrite parts. |
| **Maximum voltage** | Capacitors, switches, cables and insulation all have to be rated for the highest voltage the machine can reach. |
| **Coil or fixture design** | A standard solenoid coil is simpler than a part-specific rotor, housing or multi-pole fixture, and each additional fixture adds to the order. |
| **Cooling and cycle time** | Shorter cycles need a more powerful charger and forced-air or water-cooled fixtures. |
| **Controls and monitoring** | Basic push-button controls cost less than digital voltage setting, peak-current monitoring, data logging or a PLC with HMI. |
| **Automation** | Automatic loading, part detection, reject handling and line interfaces add hardware and engineering time. |
| **Installation and support** | On-site installation, commissioning, operator training and recommended spares are part of the total cost. |

Comparing quotations on energy rating alone can mislead: a cheaper machine that cannot saturate your most demanding part, or a fixture that overheats at your production rate, soon costs more in rejects and downtime.
