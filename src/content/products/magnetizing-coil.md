## How a magnetizing coil works

A magnetizing coil is a multi-turn copper solenoid connected to a [capacitor discharge magnet charging machine](/products/magnetizing-machines/magnet-charging-machine/). When the machine fires, a pulse of several thousand amperes passes through the winding for a few milliseconds and creates a strong field along the coil axis. Any magnet in the bore is magnetized in that direction — through the thickness of a disc or ring, or along the length of a cylinder or block.

### Pulse coil or DC magnetizing coil?

A DC magnetizing coil carries a steady current, so its field is limited by how much heat the winding can shed continuously. That is enough for alnico and some ferrite parts, but rarely for NdFeB or SmCo. A pulsed solenoid magnetizing coil carries far more current for just a few milliseconds, which is how it reaches the much higher fields that modern rare-earth magnets need without overheating.

The coil is not fussy about part shape, which makes it the most versatile tool for [loose magnet magnetizing](/applications/loose-magnet-magnetizing/). What matters is direction: anisotropic magnets, which include most sintered ferrite and NdFeB, must be loaded with their oriented direction along the coil axis or they will not reach full strength.

## Field needed by magnet material

| Magnet material | Approx. field to saturate | What it means for the coil |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alnico | Well under 1 T | Low field; most coils manage it easily |
| Ferrite | 1–1.5 T | Moderate field; larger bores and batches are practical |
| NdFeB | 2.5–3.5 T, more for high-coercivity grades | Smaller bore or more energy needed |
| SmCo | 3.5–5 T | Highest field; compact high-field coils |

A commonly quoted rule of thumb is a field of about three times the magnet's intrinsic coercivity (Hcj), but the magnet supplier's data should always take priority. Our guide to [magnetizing field requirements](/blog/magnetizing-field-requirements/) goes into more depth.

## Sizing a magnetizing coil

### Bore diameter

Choose the smallest bore that fits the largest part or batch holder with handling clearance. For the same stored energy, a smaller bore gives a stronger field, so oversizing a coil "just in case" costs field strength.

### Length and field uniformity

The field is strongest and most even near the centre of a solenoid and falls off towards the ends — at the end of a long solenoid it is about half the centre value. Parts should sit in the central zone, so the coil is made longer than the part or stack it charges.

### Batch charging

Several magnets can be charged in one pulse if they fit within the uniform zone. A non-magnetic holder keeps them in place, since magnets charged side by side push and pull on each other the moment the pulse ends. A bigger batch means a bigger bore, which needs more energy to reach the same field.

## Construction, cooling and life

Each pulse produces strong electromagnetic forces that push the winding outwards and squeeze it along its length. An industrial magnetizing coil is therefore wound with well-insulated conductor, encapsulated in resin and reinforced so the turns cannot shift. Every pulse also heats the copper: natural air cooling suits occasional charging, while forced-air or water cooling allows shorter intervals between pulses. The coil's inductance and resistance are matched to the magnetizer so the pulse stays within the ratings of both.

For radial or multi-pole patterns a solenoid is not the right tool — those parts need a [multi-pole magnetizing fixture](/products/magnetizing-fixtures-coils/multipole-magnetizing-fixture/).
