## How a rotor magnetizer works

A rotor magnetizer is a magnet charging machine for BLDC rotors: a [capacitor discharge magnetizer](/products/magnetizing-machines/magnet-charging-machine/) paired with a fixture made for one rotor design. Conductors run along the length of the fixture at every pole boundary, and the current direction alternates from one conductor to the next. When the capacitor bank fires, several kiloamperes flow for a few milliseconds and the field between each pair of neighbouring conductors drives the magnet beneath it north or south.

Because the pole pattern comes from the fixture's geometry, pole position does not depend on how an operator places pre-magnetized magnets. That is a big reason why magnetizing after assembly is standard practice in volume BLDC production — magnets can be glued and sleeved, and the rotor balanced, while everything is still unmagnetized and free of clinging steel particles. The page on [rotor magnetizing](/applications/rotor-magnetizing/) shows where this step sits on a motor line.

## Rotor types and fixture arrangements

| Rotor type | Typical magnets | Fixture arrangement | Typical motors |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Inner rotor, surface magnets | Ferrite or NdFeB arcs, or a one-piece ring, on the outside of the core | Rotor slides into the fixture bore | Pumps, blowers, servo and appliance motors |
| Outer rotor, surface magnets | Ferrite or NdFeB arcs bonded inside a steel rotor ring | Fixture fits inside the rotor | BLDC ceiling fans, e-bike and EV hub motors |
| Interior magnets (IPM) | NdFeB or ferrite blocks in lamination slots | Field must pass through the steel stack to reach the magnets | Compressor and other high-efficiency motors |

Surface-magnet rotors are the most straightforward to magnetize after assembly. IPM rotors are more demanding because the field has to reach buried magnets in the right direction, so each design needs a feasibility check before a fixture is built.

## Fan, e-mobility and EV motor rotors

### BLDC ceiling fan rotors

Most BLDC fans use an outer rotor: ferrite or NdFeB magnets, or a bonded ring, line the inside of a steel rotor shell. An inner fixture fits into the shell and forms every pole in one pulse; on arc-magnet designs, the rotor is located so each pole is centred on its segment. Fan lines run in high volumes, so cycle time and fixture cooling matter as much as field strength. Our guide on [how BLDC ceiling fan rotor magnets are magnetized](/blog/bldc-fan-rotor-magnetizing/) walks through the process step by step.

### Hub motor and e-rickshaw rotors

Hub motors for e-bikes and e-scooters are also outer-rotor designs, but larger, with many NdFeB arc magnets bonded inside the hub. Many e-rickshaw and other three-wheeler BLDC motors use similar rotors, whether hub-mounted or driving the axle. Magnetizing after bonding avoids fitting dozens of strong magnets by hand. Large diameters and high pole counts need more energy, and very large rotors may be magnetized a group of poles at a time with indexing between pulses.

### EV traction and industrial motor rotors

Inner-rotor PMSM rotors for electric vehicles, servo drives and industrial motors may use surface magnets or interior (IPM) magnets. Surface designs are usually magnetized after assembly; IPM designs are assessed individually, as described above.

## What to specify

### Pole count and alignment

A fixture produces one pole count, and it must match the motor design. On rotors built from separate arc magnets, the fixture locates the rotor from the shaft, a keyway or a pin so that every pole transition falls in the gap between two magnets rather than across one.

### Skew

Skewing the poles along the rotor length reduces cogging torque. The skew angle comes from the motor designer and is built into the conductor slots of the fixture.

### Magnet grade, pole pitch and energy

Sintered NdFeB needs a far stronger field than ferrite to saturate — roughly 2.5–3.5 T against 1–1.5 T — so the magnet grade largely decides the energy and voltage required. High pole counts on small diameters add difficulty: narrow poles leave less room for conductors, and the field does not reach as deep into the magnet. Our article on [multi-pole magnetizing](/blog/multipole-magnetizing-explained/) explains this trade-off.

### Cycle rate and checks

Output per shift decides fixture cooling — natural air for lower rates, forced air or water for continuous production. Peak-current monitoring confirms that every pulse was delivered, and a surface flux scan or back-EMF test against a reference rotor confirms the result. Together they make an industrial rotor magnetizer a controlled process step rather than just a machine on the line.

## What decides the price of a rotor magnetizer?

A rotor magnetizer is configured around one or more rotor designs, so there is no catalogue price. The main factors that decide the cost are:

| Cost driver | How it affects the price |
| --- | --- |
| **Energy rating** | Large-diameter rotors, high pole counts and high-grade NdFeB need a bigger capacitor bank than small ferrite fan rotors. |
| **Voltage** | The machine, cables and fixture insulation must all be rated for the maximum charging voltage. |
| **Fixture design** | Pole count, skew, inner or outer magnetizing, rotor location and quick-change features all add machining and design work. Each extra rotor design normally needs its own fixture. |
| **Cooling and cycle time** | Continuous fan or pump lines need faster charging and forced-air or water-cooled fixtures. |
| **Controls and monitoring** | Peak-current monitoring, data logging and an attached flux-scan or back-EMF test station add to the cost. |
| **Automation** | Pneumatic or automatic loading, part detection and PLC links to the assembly line add hardware and engineering. |
| **Installation** | On-site commissioning, operator training and spare parts form part of the total project cost. |

The useful comparison is cost per correctly magnetized rotor, not the machine price alone. A system that saturates every rotor at your line rate avoids the rejects and rework that an undersized machine or overheating fixture brings.
